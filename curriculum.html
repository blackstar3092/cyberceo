Curriculum

What we teach

We run three programs: an Introduction to Cybersecurity assembly for upper elementary students, a training workshop for senior residence staff that comes with a written guide, and an expert panel for high schoolers. Everything below is taken from the material we actually present.

The password lab and the phishing hunt are two activities from the elementary assembly. You can try both of them here.

Activity 01

The password lab

We teach that strong passwords are CLOUDS: complex, lengthy, only yours, unique, different for every site, and changed regularly. Type something in and the six checks update as you go. The crack time assumes an attacker guessing four billion times a second, which is the figure we use in the assembly.

Time to crack — Waiting
·ComplexContains numbers, symbols, and both upper and lower case letters.
·LengthyAt least 10 characters. A 6 character password can be cracked in about three minutes.
·Only yoursNot your name, your birthday, or anything someone could guess from your profile.
·UniqueNot on the common password lists. 91% of people use one of the top 1,000.
·DifferentA different password for every site, so one breach does not become all of them.
·Short termChanged every few months, and straight away after any breach.

This runs entirely in your browser. Nothing you type is sent anywhere or saved. Even so, do not paste in a password you actually use. That habit is the whole point of the lesson.

Activity 02

Spot the phish

This is the message we put on screen partway through the assembly. There are five things wrong with it. Tap each one you can find.

New message
From (Roblox Support)
To samskibidi@gmail.com (Sam Skibidi)
Subject

CLICK NOW TO CLAIM!!!

Terms and conditions apply.

0 of 5 red flags found
    That is all five. Too good to be true, all caps, a lookalike link, a request for personal information, and a spoofed sender. Any one of them on its own is enough to delete the message. You do not need to find all five to be safe.

    Elementary assembly

    Introduction to Cybersecurity

    Written for fourth and fifth graders and delivered as a full class assembly. It fits a standard class period. The order alternates explanation and games so that no section runs long enough to lose the room.

    Run of show

    01 What and why
    Cybersecurity defined, then WannaCry, the CrowdStrike outage, and Stuxnet as proof that it is not abstract.
    02 Passwords
    The CLOUDS rule, then rebuilding 1234 step by step into something that takes centuries.
    03 Password game
    One student per class spins a wheel, draws cards, and builds a password. Longest crack time wins.
    04 CIA triad
    Confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The three things security is actually protecting.
    05 How computers work
    Input, storage, memory, software, and output, plus a short tour of Windows, macOS, and Linux.
    06 Threat vocabulary
    Threat, vulnerability, exploit, and risk, and how the four fit together.
    07 Social engineering
    Phishing, spear phishing, vishing, and smishing, then the phishing hunt above.
    08 Malware
    Viruses, worms, trojans, keyloggers, botnets, backdoors, and logic bombs, plus what antivirus software does.
    09 ABCyber
    Whole class game. Spin a letter, name a cybersecurity term starting with it, and explain what it means.
    10 Cipher busters
    Decode a message in Morse code to finish.

    Malware, in the words we use

    Virus / worm
    Infects and spreads, with or without a human helping it along.
    Trojan horse
    A program with a hidden malicious function.
    Keylogger
    Tracks what you type to get passwords and personal information.
    Botnet
    Compromised computers under the control of an attacker.
    Backdoor
    A way into a program that skips the normal security checks.
    Logic bomb
    Malware that stays dormant until a specific condition is met.

    Booking one

    An assembly works for a single class or a full grade level. We supply the slides, the wheel, the cards, and the student presenters. Schools need a projector and sound. There is no cost.

    Senior residences

    Staff training and the caretaker guide

    Senior residents are one of the most targeted groups for phone, text, and email fraud, and the caretakers who support them are usually the first person a resident tells. We built the program around that.

    We run an Introduction to Cybersecurity workshop adapted for staff at senior residences, covering the scams that actually target older adults and how to walk a resident through one calmly. So far we have trained 30 staff at Villa Rancho Bernardo and 15 staff at Casa de las Campanas, alongside 15 residents.

    The workshop is only part of it. We leave every residence with a written guide so the help continues after we go home.

    Who the training is for

    Caretakers
    The staff who sit with residents day to day and get asked the questions first.
    Front desk
    Often the first to hear about a suspicious call or an unexpected visitor.
    Activity staff
    Can fold short cyber safety refreshers into programming they already run.
    Residents
    Where a residence wants us to teach residents directly, we do that too.

    Leave behind

    Teaching Cyber Safety Instructional Guide

    A written manual for senior center caretakers to refer back to after our lesson. It is built in two halves: how to protect a resident before anything happens, and exactly what to do if something already has.

    Before: protecting a resident

    • Setting up and recording strong passwords in a way a resident can actually use.
    • Turning on two step verification and account recovery options.
    • Keeping devices and apps updated.
    • Checking privacy settings on phones, tablets, and social media.
    • Recognizing the scams that target older adults most: fake grandchild calls, tech support pop ups, prize notifications, and fraudulent bills.
    • Agreeing in advance who a resident should check with before sending money or sharing information.

    After: if something has happened

    • Stop contact. Do not send anything further, and do not click anything else in the message.
    • Disconnect the device from the internet if a program was installed or remote access was given.
    • Change passwords from a different, known clean device, starting with email.
    • Call the bank or card issuer straight away to stop payments and flag the account.
    • Place a fraud alert or freeze with the credit bureaus if personal information was shared.
    • Report it. Local police, and the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov.
    • Tell the residence and the resident's family, and write down what happened while it is fresh.
    • Follow up. Scammers often come back to someone who paid once.

    The guide is free to any residence we work with. If your staff would find it useful, ask us for a copy.
